# Case Crawler

### A Legal Research Puzzle Game

*Working title — alternatives: "On Point," "Binding Authority," "The Record"*

---

## The Elevator Pitch

You're a junior associate at a law firm. A partner drops a case file on your desk and says, "Figure out if we can win this." Your job: dig through a library of cases, statutes, and legal commentary to piece together the strongest argument you can — before time runs out and the client's bill gets too high.

**Case Crawler** turns legal research into a game. Not a simulation, not a study tool — a *game*. The core loop is the part of legal work that's genuinely compelling: the detective work. Chasing a citation from one case to another. Finding a statute that changes everything. Realizing opposing counsel's star case was overruled three years ago. That moment when scattered authorities suddenly click into a coherent argument — that's the win condition.

Think of it as part puzzle game, part strategy game, part treasure hunt.

---

## How a Scenario Plays Out

### 1. The Assignment

Each scenario starts with a legal problem. It might be straightforward:

> *"Our client signed a non-compete agreement two years ago. She just left to start a competing business. Can her former employer enforce it?"*

Or it might be more complex, with multiple issues nested inside a single dispute. You get a brief fact pattern — the client's story — and your job is to research the law and build an argument.

### 2. The Research

This is where the game lives. You have access to a searchable library of fictional (but realistic) cases, statutes, regulations, and secondary sources. You can:

- **Search by keyword or topic** — just like real legal research, your search terms matter. Broad searches return noise. Narrow searches might miss the key case. Learning to search effectively is a skill the game rewards.
- **Follow citations** — cases cite other cases. Statutes reference other statutes. One of the most satisfying parts of the game is pulling on a thread: you find a case that cites an older case, which cites a statute you hadn't considered, which leads you to a regulation that changes your entire theory.
- **Read and evaluate** — not every case helps you. Some are distinguishable on the facts. Some are from jurisdictions that don't bind your court. Some have been overruled. Part of the challenge is sorting signal from noise.

### 3. Building Your Argument

As you find useful authorities, you add them to your argument. Think of this like assembling a hand of cards — each case, statute, or source is a piece, and you need to arrange them into a logical chain.

A strong argument isn't just a pile of cases. It has structure:

- A controlling statute or rule at the foundation
- Binding precedent that interprets that rule favorably
- Persuasive authority that reinforces the point
- Factual parallels between your client's situation and the cases you've found

The game rewards you for building complete, well-structured arguments — not just for finding a lot of sources.

### 4. The Opposition

Once you present your argument, opposing counsel fires back. They might:

- Cite a case you missed that cuts against you
- Distinguish your best case on the facts
- Argue that a statute you relied on doesn't apply

Now you're on the clock again. Can you find authority to rebut their points? Can you distinguish their cases? Did you miss something in your initial research that leaves you exposed?

### 5. The Ruling

The judge (or senior partner, or professor — depending on the scenario) evaluates both sides. Your score reflects:

- **Relevance** — Did your authorities actually address the legal question?
- **Strength** — Binding precedent beats persuasive authority. Recent cases beat old ones. On-point facts beat loose analogies.
- **Completeness** — Did you address all the issues, or did you leave gaps the opposition exploited?
- **Efficiency** — Did you get there within your research budget, or did you burn through billable hours chasing dead ends?

## Core Mechanics

### The Research Loop

The heart of the game is searching, reading, evaluating, and connecting. This loop should feel like detective work — each source you find either opens a new lead or closes a dead end. The game is designed so that there are multiple viable paths through each scenario, rewarding different research strategies.

### Resource Pressure

You don't have unlimited time. Each scenario gives you a budget — think of it as billable hours. Every search costs something. Every case you read costs something. This forces real tradeoffs: Do you keep digging for a perfect case, or do you go with what you have? Do you spend time trying to anticipate the opposition's argument, or shore up your own?

This isn't arbitrary time pressure — it mirrors a real constraint. Clients have budgets. Partners expect efficiency. The game turns that tension into a strategic decision.

### Authority as Currency

Legal sources function almost like cards in a strategy game. They have properties:

- **Weight** — A Supreme Court case carries more weight than a trial court opinion. A statute trumps a case. Binding authority beats persuasive authority.
- **Relevance** — A case with nearly identical facts is more valuable than one that's only loosely analogous.
- **Recency** — Newer authorities are generally stronger, and old cases might have been overruled or superseded.
- **Combinability** — Some authorities work better together. A statute plus a case interpreting that statute is stronger than either alone.

Building an argument means assembling the right combination of authorities — strong enough to withstand rebuttal, efficient enough to stay within budget.

### Distinguishing and Rebutting

Finding good law for your side is only half the game. You also need to deal with bad law. When opposing counsel throws a harmful case at you, you have options:

- **Distinguish it** — show that the facts are different enough that it shouldn't apply
- **Find something better** — locate a more recent or more authoritative case that goes your way
- **Undermine it** — maybe it's been criticized, limited, or partially overruled

This back-and-forth is where the game gets its tension.

## Progression

### Campaign Mode

A series of scenarios that escalate in complexity. Early levels introduce core concepts — what's a binding case, how citations work, why jurisdiction matters. Later levels involve multi-issue problems, conflicting lines of authority, and sophisticated opposing arguments.

The narrative could follow a career arc: law clerk to junior associate to senior associate, with each stage introducing new types of challenges. Or it could follow a single complex case through multiple stages — discovery, motions, trial, appeal — with each stage requiring different research.

### Scenario Variety

Not every scenario is a courtroom argument. Possibilities include:

- **Memo to the partner** — pure research, no opposition, scored on thoroughness
- **Client counseling** — research a question and advise a client on their options, where multiple answers might be defensible
- **Motion practice** — argue a motion to dismiss, a summary judgment motion, or an evidentiary dispute
- **Appellate argument** — work with a closed record and argue questions of law
- **Emergency research** — a judge asks a question from the bench and you have minutes, not hours, to find an answer

### Unlockables and Replayability

- **New areas of law** — start with contracts, unlock torts, constitutional law, criminal procedure, etc.
- **Harder difficulty** — less budget, tougher opposition, more ambiguous fact patterns
- **Efficiency ratings** — replay scenarios to find the argument in fewer steps
- **Hidden authorities** — some scenarios have a "golden case" that's hard to find but dramatically strengthens your argument

## Multiplayer and Competitive Possibilities

### Head-to-Head

Two players get the same fact pattern — one represents each side. Both research simultaneously, then present arguments. A scoring system (or a third player acting as judge) determines the winner. This mirrors moot court and could appeal to law students.

### Cooperative

Two players research the same side together, splitting up issues or areas of the library. This mirrors how legal research actually works in practice — associates dividing up a research memo.

### Leaderboards

For solo play, scenarios could have leaderboards based on argument strength, efficiency, or speed. "Can you build a winning argument in under 10 billable hours?"

## What It Feels Like to Play

The best analog isn't another video game — it's the feeling of solving a hard crossword or cracking a really good mystery novel. There's a specific satisfaction in legal research when the pieces come together: you've been reading cases for an hour, following threads that go nowhere, and then suddenly you find *the one* — the case that's directly on point, from the right court, with the right facts, and it cites a statute you can use to lock down the argument.

Case Crawler is built around manufacturing that moment.

The pacing alternates between exploration (searching, browsing, reading) and tension (the clock is ticking, opposing counsel just cited something devastating, the partner needs the memo by end of day). It should feel cerebral but not dry — the fictional cases can have personality, the fact patterns can be funny or dramatic or absurd, and the opposing counsel can have a character you love to beat.

For lawyers and law students, the appeal is recognition: *this is what I do, but it's actually fun now.* The game respects the craft of legal research without the soul-crushing billing pressure. For non-lawyers, the appeal is the puzzle: you don't need a law degree to enjoy connecting pieces of evidence into a coherent theory, managing limited resources, and outsmarting an opponent. The legal flavor makes it distinctive, not exclusionary.

It should feel like the best hour you ever spent in a law library — minus the flickering fluorescent lights.
