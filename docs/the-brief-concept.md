# The Brief

### A Legal Research Game — Concept Document

*Working title. Alternatives under consideration: "On the Record," "First Chair," "Due Diligence"*

---

## The Elevator Pitch

You're a junior associate at a mid-size law firm. Your phone buzzes at 11 PM — a partner needs research on a motion due tomorrow morning. The case you find (or fail to find) in the next hour will determine whether your client keeps their home, whether the partner trusts you with the next big matter, and whether you're still at the firm in six months.

**The Brief** is an interactive narrative game where legal research drives the story. Every chapter puts you in front of a new legal problem, and the research choices you make — which arguments to pursue, which authorities to rely on, which leads to follow — branch the story in meaningfully different directions. Find the landmark case that blows the opposition's argument apart, and you're the hero. Miss it, and you're not fired — but the story goes somewhere very different.

Think *Phoenix Wright* meets a legal thriller, grounded in how legal research actually works.

---

## What Does It Feel Like to Play?

Imagine settling into a chapter the way you'd settle into an episode of a good legal drama. There's a client with a real problem. There's a deadline. There's a partner breathing down your neck and opposing counsel who's sharper than you expected.

But instead of watching the lawyer on screen figure it out, *you* figure it out. You're the one reading the facts and spotting the issue. You're the one deciding whether to chase down a statutory argument or dig into case law. You're the one who finds — or doesn't find — the case that changes everything.

The game doesn't feel like a quiz. It feels like a story where you have agency. The tension comes from the same place it comes from in real practice: the clock is ticking, the answer is out there somewhere, and you have to decide where to look.

When you find the right authority, the payoff isn't a score — it's a scene. Your partner reads your memo and nods. The judge cites your case from the bench. The client calls to say thank you. And when you miss something, the story doesn't end — it bends. The motion gets denied, the case settles for less, the partner gives the next assignment to someone else. You keep playing, but the world remembers.

## How a Chapter Plays Out

A typical chapter unfolds in three acts:

### Act 1: The Setup

You receive a new assignment. Maybe a partner pulls you into her office and explains that a longtime client is being sued for breach of contract. Maybe you overhear opposing counsel's argument in the hallway and realize there's an angle no one has considered. The game sets the scene through dialogue, documents, and narrative — you understand the facts, the stakes, and what you're looking for.

### Act 2: The Research

This is the heart of the game. You're presented with a legal problem and a set of choices about how to approach it. These aren't abstract puzzles — they're framed as the kinds of decisions a real researcher makes:

- Do you start with the statute or look for case law first?
- The partner mentioned a case from ten years ago — do you track it down, or do you think there's something more recent?
- You've found a case that's helpful but from another jurisdiction. Do you use it, or keep looking for something binding?
- Opposing counsel cited *Smith v. Jones* — do you distinguish it, or do you find authority that overrules it entirely?

Each choice leads you down a different research path. Some paths lead to the strongest possible argument. Some lead to arguments that are good enough. Some lead to dead ends that cost you time you don't have. The game rewards thoroughness, intuition, and strategic thinking — but it doesn't punish you with a game-over screen. It just tells a different story.

### Act 3: The Outcome

Your research feeds directly into what happens next. If you found the right cases and built a strong argument, you watch the motion hearing play out in your favor. The dialogue changes. The judge's reaction changes. Your client's fate changes.

But here's what makes it interesting: even a "loss" is a story worth playing. If you missed a key case and the motion gets denied, the chapter doesn't end — the case pivots. Maybe you have to prep for trial instead of winning on summary judgment. Maybe the client pushes for settlement. Maybe you learn something from the loss that helps you in a later chapter. The story always moves forward.

## Story Structure

The game follows your career across a series of cases, each one a self-contained chapter with its own client, legal problem, and stakes. But the chapters aren't isolated — they build on each other in meaningful ways:

**Precedent carries forward.** A ruling you helped secure in Chapter 2 might be the authority you need in Chapter 5. If you lost that motion, that precedent doesn't exist in your story — and now Chapter 5 plays out differently.

**Relationships evolve.** The partners, associates, clients, judges, and opposing counsel you encounter have memories. The partner who trusted you with a big case after you nailed the research will give you more responsibility. The one whose motion you botched might freeze you out. Opposing counsel who lost to you last time comes in better prepared.

**Your career has a trajectory.** Early chapters establish you as a junior associate doing late-night research. As the game progresses — depending on how your cases have gone — you might be arguing motions yourself, mentoring new associates, or fighting to keep your job. The game's arc mirrors the arc of a legal career, compressed into a compelling narrative.

**The stakes escalate.** Early cases might involve a contract dispute or a small landlord-tenant matter. Later chapters could put you in the middle of a high-profile civil rights case or a corporate scandal. The legal issues get more complex, the research gets harder, and the consequences of getting it right (or wrong) get bigger.

## Core Mechanics in Plain Language

**Research as storytelling.** The central idea is that legal research decisions *are* narrative choices. Instead of choosing "go left" or "go right," you're choosing "pursue a constitutional argument" or "look for a statutory exception." The game makes these choices dramatic by showing you their consequences in the story.

**Branching paths, not pass/fail.** There is no single "correct" answer in most scenarios. There are stronger and weaker arguments, more and less relevant authorities. The game branches based on the quality and creativity of your research, not on whether you picked the one right option.

**Cumulative impact.** Your choices compound. A strong research memo early on doesn't just win one motion — it builds your reputation, earns trust, and opens doors. A weak performance doesn't end the game — it narrows your options and changes what opportunities come your way.

**Discovery and intuition.** The best moments in the game come from the same place the best moments in real research come from: the thrill of finding the case that changes everything. The game is designed to make that moment feel earned and exciting.

## What Makes the Story Compelling

Legal dramas are one of the most popular genres in fiction for a reason — the stakes are inherently human. Behind every case is someone whose life, livelihood, or liberty is on the line. **The Brief** taps into that by making sure every research problem is attached to a person and a story you care about.

But the game adds something that books and TV can't: *you did this.* When the judge rules in your client's favor, it's because of the case *you* found. When the partner promotes you, it's because of the work *you* did. And when things go wrong, you feel the weight of knowing you might have found the answer if you'd looked in the right place.

The tone walks a line between grounded realism and dramatic tension. The legal issues are real enough to feel authentic, but the narrative is crafted to be accessible and engaging. You don't need a law degree to play — the game teaches you what you need to know as part of the story. But if you *do* have a legal background, you'll recognize the problems, the pressure, and the satisfaction of getting it right.

## Who Is This For?

**The Brief** sits at an intersection that doesn't really exist yet in games:

- **Lawyers and law students** who want to see their skills reflected in a game that takes the profession seriously — not as a caricature, but as a source of genuine drama and problem-solving.
- **Fans of narrative games** (*Phoenix Wright*, *Ace Attorney*, visual novels) who are looking for something with more grounded, realistic stakes.
- **Legal thriller readers** who want to step inside the story instead of just reading it.
- **Anyone who enjoys detective/investigation games** — legal research is, at its core, investigative work. Finding the right case is a lot like finding the right clue.

The game doesn't require legal knowledge to enjoy, but it rewards it. A first-year law student might recognize a research strategy that a casual player wouldn't — and the game acknowledges that with richer story outcomes. But both players have a great time.

---

## Summary

**The Brief** is a narrative game built on a simple insight: legal research is inherently dramatic. The right case at the right time can change someone's life. This game puts that power in the player's hands and wraps it in a story worth caring about.

It's a choose-your-own-adventure where the choices are research decisions. It's a legal thriller where you're the one doing the work. It's a career story where your skills shape your future.

And it's the game that finally answers the question every junior associate has asked at 2 AM in the library: *does any of this actually matter?*

In **The Brief**, it does.
